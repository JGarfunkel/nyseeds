NYSEEDS Conservation Policy Initiative

Conservation Neighbor Partnership

A voluntary partnership of neighboring landowners who coordinate stewardship across a shared landscape.

In brief

A Conservation Neighbor Partnership invites adjoining landowners to care for their properties in coordination and to see their shared effort grow on a common map. The members are neighbors in the literal sense: their parcels touch, and together they form a continuous stretch of stewarded land.

The model is proven. We propose to begin with one neighborhood in the Croton watershed, anchored by a committed large landowner and mapped from the first day.

The idea

A Conservation Neighbor Partnership (CNP) is a voluntary association of adjoining landowners who care for their properties in coordination, guided by a shared map of their collective effort. Their parcels touch, and together they form a continuous stretch of stewarded land across a watershed, a ridgeline, or any landscape that links them.

The working model is the Aspetuck Land Trust Green Corridor in Fairfield County, Connecticut. Since 2019 Aspetuck has organized a roughly 40,000-acre area where homeowners pledge to plant native species, reduce their lawns, set aside pesticides and herbicides, and move to organic or low-emission lawn care. More than three-quarters of that land is privately owned, which places homeowners at the center of the result. Each property that takes the pledge appears on a public map marked with a chickadee, so a resident can search their address and watch participation grow around them.

A CNP takes that model and names what holds it together: neighbors, physically connected, choosing to act as one landscape. Because the parcels adjoin, a partnership spreads as a network across an area, branching in many directions at once. The terms mosaic and commons capture the same patchwork-into-whole quality, and a partnership can describe itself either way.

How a partnership works

Participation stays simple, visible, and rewarding.

A pledge and a place on the map. Joining begins with a short pledge and a property's appearance on the partnership map. The map carries the effort: it turns one household's choice into a shared, visible one and lets a neighborhood watch itself fill in, the same mechanic behind Doug Tallamy's Homegrown National Park.

Recognition and signaling. A sign or mailbox plaque, used by both Aspetuck and the regional Pollinator Pathway, lets participants show their neighbors and draw them in. Friendly challenges among neighborhoods give the effort the feel of a shared civic project.

A progression that meets people where they are. Stewardship follows short stages, so a first step asks only for attention. Washington, DC's RiverSmart Homes program offers a useful template: a property audit followed by a menu of options, including BayScaping, which presents native plantings as a stormwater measure and ties the garden work directly to water quality.

Stage 1

Understand your landscape

Learn what grows on the land and where the lot lines and public edges fall.

Stage 2

Save your trees

Relieve mature trees of the invasive vines that overwhelm them.

Stage 3

Reduce resource use

Lower fertilizer, pesticide, and irrigation use across the property.

Stage 4

Expand native habitat

Convert lawn and cleared areas to native woodland and pollinator plantings.

Anchor landowners. Large, long-lived parcels such as farms, preserves, schools, religious institutions, and clubs make natural cornerstones, holding ground for decades and lending a neighborhood credibility. An anchor steadies a partnership, and the surrounding properties follow its lead.

Brokerage to existing programs. Most landowners qualify for programs they would struggle to find on their own. A partnership connects them to easements, buffer payments, certifications, and grants, and turns membership into a path toward permanence and support.

Aggregation with privacy. The public view shows neighborhood-level totals, and owners may take part anonymously. Most property attributes already sit in the public record, so sharing a stewardship practice keeps exposure low, and the design protects it by default.

In Northern Westchester

In the Croton watershed of northern Westchester, a first partnership could organize around one of the watershed's natural sections, an area bounded by major roads for easy recognition, with subwatershed lines available as an overlay. Each section gathers a mix of homes, preserves, farms, and institutional land that already share a drainage and a set of neighbors.

A working farm such as Cabbage Hill (280 acres in Mount Kisco) makes a natural anchor, with a path into source-water programs through the Watershed Agricultural Council's East of Hudson Program (Whole Farm Planning, with technical and financial help for conservation practices), a Westchester Land Trust conservation easement for permanent protection, and federal riparian-buffer programs such as CREP where the land qualifies. The anchor steadies the section, and the surrounding homes follow with the residential-scale version of the same stewardship.

Many of these properties already sit within the Indian Brook–Croton Gorge Watershed Protection Overlay District, so a partnership gives neighbors a friendly, voluntary path toward the same water-quality goals the district pursues through regulation, and a way to see their shared progress on a map.

What a pilot asks Aspetuck resourced its corridor with a dedicated landowner-engagement director and a multi-year campaign, because shifting how people care for their yards takes sustained effort. A credible pilot needs a convener, such as a land trust, a conservation board, or a county district, and a funding source, plausibly through the Environmental Protection Fund, county support, or Hudson River Estuary watershed grants.

We propose to begin with one neighborhood, anchored by a committed large landowner and mapped from the first day, as a demonstration that thousands of small acts of stewardship add up to something a whole community can see.
